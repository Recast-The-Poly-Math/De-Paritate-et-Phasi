Testing Goldbach with Parity and Phase

A phase function from Jasmin Ivankovic’s De Paritate et Phasi gives an exact divisibility test at selected scales. The experiment uses it to filter candidate sums and verifies a prime pair for every even target from 4 through 1,000,000.

From parity to divisibility

Goldbach’s conjecture asks whether every even integer N ≥ 4 is a sum of two primes. The case 4 = 2 + 2 is immediate. For N > 4, both primes in a representation must be odd, since an even prime can only be 2 and the other summand would then be even and greater than 2.

Section 3 of De Paritate et Phasi defines the phase function ph(x) = cos(πx/h). At h = 1, the essay’s complementarity score is zero for every integer split of an even target, because the two summands always have the same parity. Scaling the phase by h = q/2 gives a test for a different property. Read §3 of the original essay (PDF).

pq/2(x) = cos(2πx/q) = 1 if and only if q divides x.

For each odd a with 3 ≤ a ≤ N/2, take b = N − a. For an odd prime q, reject the split if either summand is a multiple of q other than q itself. Integer remainders evaluate this condition exactly.

The exact cutoff

It suffices to check all odd primes q ≤ √(N − 3). Both summands are at most N − 3, and every odd composite has an odd prime divisor at most its square root. Every prime summand survives every check. Thus a split survives all checks through this cutoff exactly when both summands are prime. This is trial division expressed in the paper’s phase notation.

For instance, 1,000 = 11 + 989 survives the four listed checks. The phase at h = 23/2 rejects it, since 989 = 23 × 43.

The full phase filter agreed with trial division on every odd split of every even target from 6 through 2,000. A separate exhaustive run used an Eratosthenes sieve to find one prime pair for each of the 499,999 even targets from 4 through 1,000,000. Trial division independently checked both primes in every reported pair.

What a proof still requires

Any fixed finite list of phase checks will miss some composites. Let M be twice the product of the tested odd primes. The odd composite (M + 1)2 has remainder 1 modulo every tested prime. Taking it as both summands therefore gives a composite pair that passes all those checks.

A proof would require a uniform lower bound of at least one surviving split after the cutoff grows with N. The experiment gives a reproducible demonstration of the phase method on a finite range. Oliveira e Silva, Herzog, and Pardi previously verified Goldbach through 4 × 1018. Mathematics of Computation, 2014.
